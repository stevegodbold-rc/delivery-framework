# First-30-Days Delivery Diagnostic Checklist
### Qualifying an organisation against the Delivery Management Framework

**How to use this.** This is not a framework-compliance audit. Its job is to tell you, fast, three things: (1) what the organisation actually does versus what it says it does, (2) which gaps are process gaps you can fix in a quarter versus structural/political ones that take years or a reorg, and (3) what needs to go in your first leadership update versus what you quietly start fixing without announcing it. Treat every answer you get as a claim to be verified against an artefact or a second source, not a fact.

Framework documents referenced: [readme.md](readme.md), [delivery-portfolio-management.md](delivery-portfolio-management.md), [stage-1-sales-to-delivery-transition.md](stage-1-sales-to-delivery-transition.md) through [stage-4-delivery-to-finance-transition.md](stage-4-delivery-to-finance-transition.md), [role-position-descriptions.md](role-position-descriptions.md), [adoption-tiers.html](adoption-tiers.html), [knowledge-management.md](knowledge-management.md).

## Week 1 — Establish the ground truth before anyone briefs you

The point of week 1 is not to learn the org chart people hand you. It's to find where the org chart and the real accountability lines diverge, and to get your hands on primary artefacts before anyone has time to tidy them.

**1.1 — Pull the artefacts, not the narrative.**
- Ask for the last three months of: Engagement Health Register entries, Exception Decision Records, Closure Acknowledgements, Finance Handover Records. Don't ask "do you do health checks" — ask to see the actual register. A framework with a fortnightly health check cadence on paper and a register that hasn't been touched in six weeks tells you everything about whether this is a *lived* framework or a *documented* one.
- Count how many engagements currently show a written rationale versus a copy-pasted one across fortnights. The adoption-tiers document names this exact failure mode — "rationale-templating" — as the tell that a cadence has decayed into theatre. If most Green ratings read like "on track, no issues" verbatim across four fortnights, the health score is decorative, not diagnostic.
- Get the actual current bench list and the actual active-engagements-per-Delivery-Owner count. Don't wait for the monthly Portfolio Health Review to show you — ask Resource Management directly, day one, because bench time and load figures are exactly the numbers that get softened before they reach a new leader.

**1.2 — Find out who actually holds the Delivery Owner role, and how many hats they wear.**
- Map real people against Delivery Owner, Practice Lead, Resource Manager, Finance Partner, Delivery Director. At sub-~50-person scale the framework itself expects one person to hold two or three of these — that's fine. What's not fine, and what you need to know within days, is if the *same* person holds Delivery Owner and Practice Lead on the same engagement (self-review with no check), or if the Resource Manager function doesn't exist at all and utilisation is being managed from memory.
- Ask each Delivery Owner-equivalent how many concurrent engagements they're carrying, unweighted. Compare to what leadership believes. This is your first flow-management gap check: if nobody can tell you the complexity-weighted load per DO, the WIP ceiling the framework treats as load-bearing at every tier doesn't exist yet, no matter what the deck says.

**1.3 — Find the informal escalation network.**
- Ask two or three experienced people (not the ones handed to you as your "guides"): "When an engagement is genuinely going wrong, who actually finds out first, and how?" The answer is rarely "the fortnightly health check." It's usually a Slack message, a corridor conversation, or a Finance Partner noticing an invoice dispute. That informal network is your real Recovery and Exception trigger mechanism today — the framework's formal trigger conditions (Red held two fortnights, EAC breach, funding signal) are a target state, not necessarily the current one.
- This also tells you where the political risk lives. If escalation currently runs through a founder or a senior partner outside any documented forum, that person is your real Delivery Director whether or not the title exists, and any framework rollout that doesn't route through them will be quietly ignored.

**1.4 — Read three closed engagements end to end, cold.**
- Pick one that leadership calls a clean win, one that was "difficult but recovered," and one that ended badly or churned a client. Trace them through what artefacts exist: was there a Delivery Handover Record, an Exception Decision Record if the trigger conditions were plausibly met, a client closure meeting, a retrospective? Gaps here are your baseline maturity reading — more reliable than any self-assessment you'll be given, and this is exactly the exercise the framework's own Win Review is built to formalise later. You're doing an ad hoc version of it in week 1 because you don't have a year of paper trail yet to run the real thing.

**1.5 — Identify the revenue model and delivery vehicle mix before you assume the framework's assumptions hold.**
- Fixed-price, T&M, managed service/retainer, and staff-augmentation books behave completely differently under this framework. A staff-aug-heavy book (bodies against a client-run backlog, no delivery vehicle profile, no Delivery Owner accountable for scope) doesn't map cleanly onto Gate 1/2/3 at all — the framework's gates assume the organisation owns delivery accountability for an engagement, not that it's placing people into someone else's team. If a meaningful slice of revenue is staff-aug, flag that as a framework-fit gap early; don't try to force Gate 2 exit criteria ("all contracted deliverables completed or formally accepted") onto an engagement type that has no deliverables to accept.
- Also check: does the organisation run any recurring managed-service/retainer book already at scale? The framework's own gap list (adoption-tiers.html) names the absence of a renewal gate, SLA health dimensions, and an ARR/churn view as unbuilt at every tier. If retainer revenue is already material — not a future-state concern — this is a genuine structural gap you will have to design around yourself, not something you're behind on adopting.

## Weeks 2–3 — Test the mechanisms under load, not on paper

By now you should be testing whether the org's actual behaviour holds up the way the framework's mechanisms assume it will, and separating "this is broken" from "this hasn't been built yet" from "this is political."

**2.1 — Stress-test the health-score rollup against incentive, not design.**
- The framework's own rollup logic (Amber if any dimension is Amber, Red if any is Red or two-plus Amber) is sound on paper. Test whether it survives contact with a Delivery Owner whose bonus, reputation, or account growth story depends on the rating. Ask: in the last two quarters, has any engagement been overridden from the computed rollup to a better rating, and was the override documented with a real rationale or a thin one? On a non-strategic account, that override currently has *no visibility above the Delivery Owner* — the framework only forces Portfolio Health Review visibility on strategic-account overrides. That means the self-rating problem the framework explicitly designed the EAC/runway checks to counter is only checked on the accounts big enough to be called strategic. On everything else, you're trusting the self-rating unless you go looking. Expect this to be the single most common source of a false-Green portfolio.
- Under a **high-growth, aggressive-hiring quarter**: expect Amber/Red ratings to be softened more than usual, because a Delivery Owner juggling new joiners and bench pressure has every incentive to avoid triggering an Exception Review that pulls Delivery Director attention onto an engagement that's merely stretched, not failing. Watch for this specifically if you're walking into a growth phase.
- Under **single-client concentration**: watch for the opposite failure — an engagement kept quietly Green well past the point of real risk because nobody wants to be the person who tells a Managing Director their biggest account just went Red. This is where the framework's strategic-account moderation (Practice Lead co-review, override visibility at Portfolio Health Review) is supposed to help — check whether it's actually happening, or whether the "strategic account" definition (an account that justifies most of one DO's ceiling) has even been operationalised. If nobody can name which accounts meet that definition, the moderation mechanism doesn't exist regardless of what the document says.

**2.2 — Test Recovery and Exception as a real forum, not a name.**
- Ask for the last five Exception Decision Records. Do they exist? Do they show a trigger condition, a rationale, and a follow-up date that was actually honoured, or does "Recovery and Exception status" become a place engagements go to die quietly? The framework itself names this exact failure mode — a Recovery and Exception entry becoming "a permanent, unexamined feature of the portfolio view" — as a governance failure in its own right. Check days-in-status, not just count-in-status.
- Ask what happens when a Delivery Owner leaves mid-engagement while an account is Red or strategic. The framework has no defined succession mechanism here (this is on its own gap list) — so this is a pure test of organisational maturity, not framework compliance. How the org has handled this in the past (ad hoc, painfully, or with a real handover process) tells you a lot about resilience under stress that no metric will show you.

**2.3 — Map real team shapes against the role model, and look for load and boundary problems.**
- The framework's role set (Delivery Owner, Practice Lead, Resource Manager, Project Manager/Lead Consultant, Finance Partner, Delivery Director) reads as a fairly flat, engagement-centred structure — closer to a set of accountable individuals than to distinct team types with different interaction modes. That's a reasonable simplification at 50-120 people. It starts to strain once specialisation shows up. Check for:
  - A **complicated-subsystem problem hiding inside a Delivery Owner's book**: is there a person or small group (a data migration specialist, an integration architect, a security lead) that every engagement quietly routes through, with no rotation and no defined interaction mode? If so, that person has become a de facto platform/complicated-subsystem team of one, invisible to the WIP ceiling, and a single point of failure the framework's Delivery Owner load ceiling doesn't see because their time isn't counted as an "engagement."
  - **Cognitive load on the Delivery Owner role itself.** The framework asks one person to hold client relationship, commercial health, workforce fit, scope, and forecast confidence simultaneously, across potentially several engagements weighted by complexity. Ask current Delivery Owners, directly, whether they feel like they're doing five jobs. If the honest answer is yes even before you add framework overhead, the framework's own stated ~15-20% "honesty ceiling" on Delivery Owner load (named in the adoption-tiers advance-gate criteria) is a real constraint you need to respect, not a nice-to-have.
  - **Unclear boundary between Delivery Owner and Project Manager/Lead Consultant.** The framework separates "overall accountability" (DO) from "day-to-day delivery management" (PM/Lead Consultant) but in a lot of real orgs at this scale these collapse into one person, or worse, into an unspoken tug-of-war where the PM runs the engagement and the DO shows up for governance theatre. Find out which it actually is per engagement.

**2.4 — Check whether flow is actually managed, or just measured after the fact.**
- The framework is explicit — refreshingly so — that most of its portfolio measures (utilisation, bench time, lead times) were snapshots or averages until the flow-management section added queues, limits, and pull policies on top. Test whether that layer is real in this organisation or still aspirational:
  - Is there an actual enforced WIP ceiling per Delivery Owner, or does "ceiling" mean "the number Delivery Director remembers from the last conversation"?
  - Is the bench genuinely treated as a managed queue with an aging threshold and an owner, or does bench time get discovered retrospectively at month-end?
  - Does a hiring requisition queue exist with a WIP limit, or does the practice post reqs freely and let time-to-fill blow out silently?
  - If none of this exists, that's not necessarily a crisis — it may be entirely appropriate for the org's current scale (Tier 1 Core in the framework's own tiering only asks for one WIP limit: the Delivery Owner load ceiling). The diagnostic question is whether the org is *pretending* to Tier 2/3 flow discipline in its reporting deck while actually operating on Tier 0 snapshots. That mismatch — claimed maturity versus operating reality — is the single most useful thing you can surface in this phase.

**2.5 — Find the shadow reporting structures.**
- Wherever engagements plausibly run Scrum or a lightweight Kanban model, check whether the fortnightly health check is genuinely drawing on the team's own ceremony outputs (sprint review, retrospective, velocity/burndown, cycle time) as the framework instructs, or whether a Delivery Owner is independently reconstructing a status view the team doesn't recognise. The framework is explicit that this second pattern is exactly what it's trying to prevent — a shadow reporting channel competing with the team's own inspect-and-adapt loop. In practice this is very common: a DO under portfolio pressure to produce a rating on a fixed fortnightly clock, regardless of where the sprint boundary actually falls, will default to inventing a number rather than waiting for the team's real cadence. Ask a Scrum Master or delivery lead directly whether they've ever been asked for a status update that didn't correspond to anything the team itself reported. If yes, that's an active shadow-reporting problem, not a theoretical risk.
- Wherever a client mandates PRINCE2 or a formal stage-gated methodology, check whether the framework's Gate 1/2/3 have been mapped onto the client's actual stage boundaries and tolerances, or whether delivery is quietly running two governance structures — the client's Project Board cadence and the framework's own gates — with nobody consciously deciding how they relate. The framework's own methodology-interface guidance says the client's cadence should be authoritative and the framework's checks should consume its artefacts (Highlight Reports, RAID logs) as evidence, not duplicate them. Verify this is happening in practice on at least one PRINCE2-governed engagement. If the framework's gates and the client's stage boundaries are being run as two separate parallel tracks, that's real bureaucratic overhead landing on delivery teams for no added control — worth naming specifically, because it's the kind of thing that erodes goodwill toward any framework fast.

**2.6 — Look for the gaps the framework itself admits to.**
The adoption-tiers document is unusually candid about what's not yet designed at any tier. Use that list as a direct diagnostic checklist against this specific organisation, because these are exactly the places a real portfolio will produce surprises the framework can't yet catch:
- Is there any managed-services/retainer renewal mechanism, or does renewal risk currently surface only when a client fails to renew?
- Is there a capacity contraction path, or does the org only have muscle memory for scaling up? If you're walking into a business that's about to face a downturn or a client loss, this gap is not academic — ask directly how the last headcount reduction was handled, because you will likely have to build this mechanism yourself.
- Is there a subcontractor/partner co-delivery model in the workforce data at all? If 10-30% of delivery capacity runs through partners or subcontractors (common at this scale) and the utilisation, quality, and margin machinery has zero visibility into it, that's a real blind spot in the numbers you're about to be handed, not a rounding error.
- Is there a formal dispute path distinct from mutual early termination? A client-initiated contractual dispute is a materially different animal from an agreed early exit, and if the org has never separated these, find out how the last dispute was actually run — usually by legal and a senior partner working around whatever delivery governance existed, which tells you where real authority sits under stress.

## Week 4 — Synthesise: tier reality, risk register, and what to say versus what to just fix

**4.1 — Place the org honestly against the adoption tiers, and be prepared for the answer to be uneven, not clean.**
Score each dimension independently rather than assigning one blended tier — real organisations are rarely coherent across all of them:
- Stages & gates: does a Delivery Handover Record, Deal Quality score, Closure Acknowledgement, and Finance Handover Record exist and get used per engagement, or only on the engagements someone remembered to be careful about?
- Engagement control: is the fortnightly health check evidence-forced (EAC/runway checks) or self-rated theatre?
- Portfolio: does one real forum exist with the right chairs in the room, or is the "monthly portfolio review" actually four disconnected conversations wearing one name?
- Flow: is there a genuine WIP ceiling anywhere, or only measurement after the fact?
- Knowledge: does anything survive a person leaving, or does institutional memory walk out the door with every departure?

It is entirely normal, and worth stating plainly to leadership, that an organisation can be Tier 2 on gates and Tier 0 on flow management simultaneously. Don't round to a single number for the sake of a tidy slide — that tidiness will be wrong the first time someone tests it.

**4.2 — Separate genuine process gap from political risk from structural limit.**
For every gap you found in weeks 1-3, classify it:
- **Process gap** (fixable in a quarter, no reorg needed): the health check exists but rationales are copy-pasted; the bench isn't tracked as a queue; requisitions have no WIP limit. These are worth quietly starting to fix now — get an evidence-forced check running, start a simple bench queue view — without waiting for a big rollout announcement. Small, visible wins here build credibility fast.
- **Political risk** (a person or incentive structure, not a missing document): the real escalation path runs around a senior partner who doesn't want bad news formalised; a Delivery Owner's bonus depends on the account they rate; a Red engagement with a client who doesn't yet know it's Red because nobody has told them and nobody wants to be first. These need to go into your first conversation with your own leadership — not as an accusation, but as a named risk with a plan, because if you sit on a known-Red engagement that surfaces publicly later, that's now your accountability, not the previous regime's.
- **Structural limit** (needs a reorg, a new role, or a multi-quarter build): sub-portfolio construct for a Delivery Director who is single-threaded past ~10 Delivery Owners; a genuine platform/complicated-subsystem team hiding inside one person's day job; a renewal mechanism for a retainer book that doesn't exist yet. Name these honestly as multi-quarter, and don't let anyone — including yourself — pretend a framework rollout alone fixes them.

**4.3 — Early warning signs worth flagging in week 4, specifically:**
- Any engagement where the real status (from your artefact reads and informal conversations) is materially worse than the reported status. Flag this immediately and specifically — don't generalise it into "reporting could be tighter." Name the engagement.
- Any account concentration where a single client represents enough revenue or reputational exposure that its delivery risk is effectively the organisation's risk, and where the account has no named successor Delivery Owner or documented account record. This is a business-continuity flag, not a delivery-process flag, and it belongs with the Managing Director, not buried in a delivery deck.
- Any pattern of unagreed scope drift on fixed-price work — informal "while you're at it" work absorbed outside change control. This is margin leakage happening silently right now, and the framework treats it correctly as a red flag, but only if someone is actually looking for it. If nobody currently is, say so.
- Any hiring freeze or downturn signal colliding with a bench that has no managed queue view. This is where flow-management gaps turn into cash-burn fastest, and it's worth flagging even if the org's numbers look fine today, because the mechanism to see it coming doesn't yet exist.

**4.4 — What to quietly start fixing without a leadership announcement:**
- Evidence-forcing the health check (require the EAC/runway check behind any Amber/Red self-rating) — this can start next fortnight with the Delivery Owners you already trust, without a framework-wide mandate.
- A simple bench-queue view, even a spreadsheet, read alongside remaining capacity — cheap, immediately useful, no political cost.
- A one-page Account Delivery Record for your two or three largest accounts, even ahead of a formal multi-engagement trigger — the key-person risk it addresses is real today, not a Tier 2 nice-to-have.
- Naming, informally, who the de facto complicated-subsystem specialists are and starting a conversation about rotation — long before you attempt any formal team-topology redesign.

## Explicit gaps in the framework itself, relevant to a first-30-days diagnostic

Worth knowing going in, because the framework won't hand you tooling for these and a diagnostic that assumes it will is a diagnostic that will miss real risk:

- **No managed-services renewal gate.** If you're joining an org with meaningful recurring/retainer revenue, you are diagnosing and likely designing this yourself — the framework has no SLA health dimension, no ARR/churn view, no renewal checkpoint at any tier.
- **No capacity contraction mechanism.** The framework's flow management is hiring-shaped only. If you're walking into a downturn, cost pressure, or a client loss that requires bench reduction, there is no defined queue, forum, or owner for that motion — you will need to build one, and fast, if conditions require it.
- **No formal dispute path distinct from mutual early termination.** If the organisation has active or recent client disputes, don't expect the framework's Exception Review / early-termination language to map cleanly — a contested dispute is a different animal (often legal-led) and the framework doesn't yet distinguish it.
- **No subcontractor/partner co-delivery treatment.** If subcontracted capacity is material, the utilisation, margin, and quality numbers you're handed in week 1 are probably understating true delivery risk and cost — this is invisible in the framework's workforce measures entirely.
- **No Delivery Owner succession mechanism.** Departure mid-engagement, especially on a Red or strategic account, triggers nothing defined. Worth testing directly: ask what happened the last time this occurred.
- **Sub-portfolio construct not designed.** If the Delivery Director (or equivalent) role you're inheriting or stepping into is already coordinating more than ~10 Delivery Owners, the framework itself flags this as a load the current design can't carry — the position description's "delegate the chairing load" line is an acknowledgement, not a mechanism.
- **Strategic-account delegation-of-authority gap.** The framework notes decisions on strategic accounts should be value-banded with the Managing Director informed rather than deciding on everything below threshold, and that this isn't yet fixed. Worth checking whether, in practice, every strategic-account decision — however small — is currently routing to the most senior person by habit, which will not scale and may already be a bottleneck.
