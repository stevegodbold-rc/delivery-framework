# Discovery Interview Scripts
### Structured conversations for the First-30-Days Delivery Diagnostic

**How to use this.** This document is a companion to [first-30-days-diagnostic-checklist.md](first-30-days-diagnostic-checklist.md). The checklist tells you what to go and find out during weeks 1-4; these six scripts are the actual conversations you have to get there. Each script is written for a specific stakeholder type you'll meet during discovery, and each follows the same shape: an opening framing to set the terms of the conversation, a set of core questions, and a rehearsed-answer probe under each one — because the first answer you get in a discovery conversation is very often the tidy, coached one, and the probe is what gets you past it.

These scripts assume you are running the First-30-Days diagnostic and are not a substitute for it — read that document first. Treat every answer here the same way the checklist tells you to treat every artefact: as a claim to verify, not a fact.

Framework documents referenced: [first-30-days-diagnostic-checklist.md](first-30-days-diagnostic-checklist.md), [readme.md](readme.md), [delivery-portfolio-management.md](delivery-portfolio-management.md), [stage-2-project-engagement-delivery.md](stage-2-project-engagement-delivery.md), [role-position-descriptions.md](role-position-descriptions.md).

## Script 1 — Delivery Owner (or closest equivalent)

**Opening framing.** Do this 1:1, with no Practice Lead or Resource Manager in the room, and say so out loud: "This isn't a performance conversation and nothing you tell me goes back to your rating history. I'm trying to understand how the job actually works day to day, not how it's described in the handbook. I'd rather hear it messy than hear it tidy." If they've clearly been coached ("just tell them it's all fine"), name that gently: "I know some of you have probably been told to put your best foot forward with me — I'd genuinely rather you didn't."

1. "Walk me through your current book — how many engagements, and what does each one actually take from you day to day?"
   *Probe if too clean:* If they give you a count and stop, ask them to weight it themselves: "If you had to rank these by how much headspace they actually take, not their contract value, what's the order?" People who've never been asked this hesitate — that hesitation is data.

2. "On the ones that are Green right now, are they Green because they're genuinely fine, or Green because you haven't had a reason to look hard at them this fortnight?"
   *Probe:* If they say "no, they're properly fine," ask for the last EAC/runway check behind one of them, specifically. "Can you show me the actual numbers behind that Green, not just the rating?" If they can't produce it in the room, that's your answer regardless of what they said.

3. "Tell me about a time in the last two quarters you rated something Amber or Red and it caused you a problem — with the client, with leadership, with your own numbers."
   *Probe:* If the answer is "never had an issue," that's a flag, not reassurance. Ask directly: "So nothing's ever been softened before it got submitted?" Watch the pause.

4. "If an engagement went properly bad tomorrow — Red, client escalation, the works — who finds out first, and how?"
   *Probe:* If they say "the fortnightly health check would catch it," push: "Realistically though — Slack message, phone call, or the register?" You want the honest informal channel, not the documented one.

5. "Where does your job stop and your PM/Lead Consultant's job start on your busiest engagement?"
   *Probe:* If the boundary sounds textbook ("I own accountability, they own day-to-day"), ask for a concrete recent example: "Give me the last decision that was genuinely ambiguous — whose call was it, and how did you two sort it out?"

6. "Is there anyone — a specialist, an architect, someone technical — that every engagement of yours ends up routing through no matter what?"
   *Probe:* If they name someone and then immediately downplay it ("oh, but that's just how it's always worked"), that's your complicated-subsystem-of-one flag. Ask: "What happens to your delivery timeline if that person is out for three weeks?"

7. "Honestly — does this feel like one job or several right now?"
   *Probe:* If they laugh it off ("it's a lot but I manage"), don't let it go: "If I took one thing off your plate, what would it be?" A real answer here tells you more than any load spreadsheet.

8. "When did you last escalate something and regret it — either because nothing happened, or because something happened to you for raising it?"
   *Probe:* This is the incentive-honesty question. If they can't think of an example either way, ask: "So has anyone here ever actually been burned for calling something Red?" The answer shapes every other conversation you'll have this month.

## Script 2 — Resource Manager / bench and requisition owner

**Opening framing.** Frame this as a systems conversation, not a headcount conversation: "I'm less interested in the current numbers than in how you'd know if they were about to go wrong. Talk me through the mechanics, not the dashboard." This disarms people who expect to be asked to defend a bench percentage.

1. "Walk me through what happens the day someone rolls off an engagement — from the moment you know, to the moment they're staffed on the next thing."
   *Probe if too clean:* If it sounds like a smooth pipeline, ask for a name: "Give me an actual person on the bench right now — how long have they been there, and what's the plan for them this week, specifically?"

2. "Is there a limit on how many open requisitions the practice can run at once, or do we post freely and see what happens?"
   *Probe:* If they say there's a limit, ask who enforces it and what happens when it's breached: "Has it ever actually stopped a req from going out?" If the answer is "not really," the ceiling is decorative.

3. "How do you find out someone's been sitting on the bench too long — do you see it as it happens, or find out at month-end?"
   *Probe:* Ask them to show you the artefact, not describe it: "Can you pull that view up right now?" If it takes more than a minute or requires reconstructing from memory or email, it's not a managed queue.

4. "When a Delivery Owner tells you their load, do you take that number at face value, or do you have a way of checking it?"
   *Probe:* If they say they trust the Delivery Owner's number, ask: "Has a Delivery Owner's stated load ever turned out to be wrong, and how did you find out?" You're listening for whether discovery was proactive or accidental.

5. "If we hit a hiring freeze tomorrow, what would you do with the current bench and the open reqs?"
   *Probe:* If the answer is vague ("we'd figure it out"), that's the capacity-contraction gap surfacing live. Ask: "Has that actually happened before here? Walk me through what you did."

6. "How much of our delivery capacity right now is subcontractors or partners, and where does that show up in your numbers?"
   *Probe:* If they say "not much" without a number, push for the number. If they genuinely don't track it, name it plainly: "So if 20% of delivery is subcontracted, that's currently invisible to you?"

7. "Tell me about the last time a Delivery Owner and you disagreed about whether someone was available."
   *Probe:* If "that doesn't really happen," ask: "So load and availability data always match between you and the Delivery Owners? Every time?" Nobody's data is that clean — a too-quick yes means they haven't been asked to reconcile it recently.

8. "If I asked you right now for complexity-weighted load per Delivery Owner — not headcount, actual weighted load — could you give it to me?"
   *Probe:* If yes, ask to see the artefact on the spot. If no, don't treat it as a failure — just name it: "So that's being tracked informally, or not at all?" — and move on without making them defend the gap.

## Script 3 — Practice Lead / senior delivery leadership peer

**Opening framing.** This is a peer conversation, so play it as one: "I'm not going to ask you to grade your own team — I want your read on the organisation as someone who's been here longer than me. Where does the real influence sit, versus the chart?" Senior peers relax when you position them as informant-on-the-system, not subject-of-review.

1. "If an engagement is genuinely going wrong, who finds out first — realistically, not on paper?"
   *Probe if too clean:* If they say "the health check process," push: "When's the last time that's actually how it happened, versus someone hearing it in a corridor or a call?"

2. "Who in this business, regardless of title, is the person people actually go to when something's about to blow up?"
   *Probe:* If they hesitate or deflect ("we're pretty flat, everyone talks to everyone"), name the pattern you're testing for directly: "I'm asking because I want to know if there's a de facto Delivery Director who isn't on the org chart — that's not a criticism, I just need to know who it is."

3. "Tell me about a strategic account where the rating didn't match reality for a while. What actually happened, and how did it surface?"
   *Probe:* If "we don't really have that problem," ask them to name the two or three accounts that would count as strategic under the framework's definition — the ones that justify most of a Delivery Owner's ceiling. If they can't name them cleanly, the moderation mechanism isn't operating regardless of what's documented.

4. "When a Delivery Owner leaves mid-engagement on a Red or strategic account, what actually happens? Not what should happen — what has happened."
   *Probe:* Ask for a specific instance. If they can only describe it in the abstract, that's a sign it hasn't really been tested, or it went badly enough that nobody wants to walk through it in detail.

5. "Where do you think the biggest political risk in the current portfolio sits — not the biggest delivery risk, the biggest political one?"
   *Probe:* If they answer only in delivery terms, redirect: "I mean specifically — is there an account, or a person, where naming the real status would cause a problem for someone senior?"

6. "Has anyone ever overridden a computed health rating to something better, and did that get real scrutiny or a rubber stamp?"
   *Probe:* If "overrides are rare and always justified," ask for the last one they personally know of and what the rationale actually said, word for word if they remember it.

7. "If you were advising me privately — not for the record — what's the one thing in this portfolio you'd tell me to go look at myself rather than trust the reporting on?"
   *Probe:* This question rarely needs a probe — if they dodge it entirely, that itself is the signal, and worth noting who declines to answer it.

8. "How many Delivery Owners realistically report into the Delivery Director layer today, and does that person actually have visibility into all of them, or just the loud ones?"
   *Probe:* If they say "all of them, obviously," ask: "When's the last time [Delivery Director] personally dug into one of the quiet ones?"

## Script 4 — Finance Partner

**Opening framing.** Finance people respond well to being treated as the source of ground truth rather than a support function: "I trust your numbers more than I trust the narrative I've been given so far. I want to look at margin and disputes through your eyes, not through the delivery deck." This also flatters accurately — Finance usually does see the real picture first.

1. "Which engagements are currently bleeding margin, and did that show up in your numbers before or after it showed up in the Engagement Health Register?"
   *Probe if too clean:* If "they always match up," ask for a specific recent example where margin moved and trace whether the health rating moved with it or lagged.

2. "Show me an EAC that turned out to be wrong. What did the real number end up being, and who was surprised?"
   *Probe:* If they claim EACs are generally accurate, ask: "So no engagement has ever quietly run over on estimate-at-completion without Delivery flagging it first?" Push for a specific counter-example if their first answer is a blanket yes.

3. "On fixed-price work, are you seeing scope drift — informal 'while you're at it' work — hit the books without a change order behind it?"
   *Probe:* If "change control is followed," ask them to pull a fixed-price engagement's actuals against its original SOW hours and see if the two line up cleanly. Offer to look at it together if they hesitate.

4. "Tell me about the last client dispute — a real contested one, not a mutual early exit. Who actually ran it, and did delivery governance have a role or did it go straight to legal and a partner?"
   *Probe:* If "we haven't really had one," ask about the last invoice that was disputed or delayed for cause, even informally — disputes often exist below the level of "formal legal dispute."

5. "Is there a client or engagement right now where you personally have more concern than the current health rating reflects?"
   *Probe:* This is your direct false-Green test. If they say no, ask: "If you had to bet, which account's rating would you least want to stake your own credibility on?" — reframing as a bet often gets past a polite "no."

6. "How much of our revenue is retainer or managed-service, and what do you currently see as the renewal risk on that book?"
   *Probe:* If they don't have a clean answer, that itself confirms there's no renewal-risk view in the numbers — worth stating plainly rather than pushing further.

7. "Do you have visibility into subcontractor or partner costs against the margin on engagements that use them, or is that a blind spot?"
   *Probe:* If "we account for it fine," ask to see one engagement's true margin including partner cost passed through — sometimes the accounting exists but nobody's actually pulled the two views together to look at true delivered margin.

8. "If I asked you to name the two engagements you'd worry about most if I disappeared for a quarter, which would they be, and why?"
   *Probe:* If they name the same accounts leadership already flagged to you, that's convergence — reassuring. If they name different ones, that divergence is exactly what week 4 needs to surface.

## Script 5 — Frontline delivery staff (Project Manager/Lead Consultant, Scrum Master, engineer)

**Opening framing.** This group is the most likely to perform if they think anything gets back to their Delivery Owner. Say so explicitly and mean it: "Nothing you say here goes back with your name on it, and I'm not evaluating your engagement's rating — I'm trying to understand whether the reporting reflects what your team actually experiences." Keep it informal, ideally over coffee rather than a booked meeting room.

1. "When was the last time you were asked for a status update that didn't line up with what the team had actually just said in retro or sprint review?"
   *Probe if too clean:* If "that never happens, the Delivery Owner just uses our numbers," ask them to walk you through the last fortnightly health check conversation specifically — who asked what, and where the numbers in it actually came from.

2. "Does your Delivery Owner or PM ever ask you for a number on a day that doesn't match your sprint boundary?"
   *Probe:* If yes but downplayed ("it's not a big deal, I just estimate"), ask: "So sometimes the rating in the register is basically your best guess mid-sprint rather than something the team actually reported?" Get them to confirm plainly.

3. "How's the team actually doing right now — not the rating, the real answer?"
   *Probe:* If it doesn't match the current Engagement Health Register colour at all, ask directly: "Does [Delivery Owner/PM] know it feels like that, or is that not really surfaced?"

4. "Tell me about the last retrospective — what came out of it, and did anything change as a result?"
   *Probe:* If "we don't really do retros regularly," that's worth noting plainly rather than probing further — it tells you the ceremony infrastructure the health check is meant to draw on may not exist.

5. "Is there someone on this engagement — a specialist, an architect, a particular expert — who everything seems to route through no matter what?"
   *Probe:* If yes, ask: "What happens when they're on leave?" Frontline staff often know the answer to this before leadership does.

6. "If something was going properly wrong on this engagement, would you tell your Delivery Owner directly, or would it go through someone else first?"
   *Probe:* If "straight to the Delivery Owner, no problem," ask for the last time that actually happened and what the Delivery Owner did with it.

7. "Do you feel like the client sees the same picture your team sees, or is there a gap between what we tell them and what's actually going on?"
   *Probe:* If "pretty much the same," push gently: "Has the client ever been surprised by something that the team already knew was coming?"

8. "If you could change one thing about how status gets reported upward from this team, what would it be?"
   *Probe:* Rarely needs a probe — a strong, specific, immediate answer here is usually the most honest thing you'll hear all month. A vague "it's all fine" is itself the signal to come back to this person separately, one-on-one, later.

## Script 6 — Leadership sponsor (whoever hired you) — Week 4 checkpoint

**Opening framing.** This conversation is different in kind — you're not extracting truth from someone performing, you're calibrating with someone who has skin in the outcome. Frame it as a two-way exchange, not a report-out: "I want to tell you what I found, but first I want to hear what you already suspected, so I know where I'm confirming something versus surfacing something new." This also protects you politically — you want their prior on record before you say anything, not after.

1. "Before I tell you anything — which engagements or people were you already worried about coming into this?"
   *Probe if too clean:* If "none really, I brought you in to formalise things, not fix a fire," push once, directly: "So if I told you there's an account that's effectively Red right now and the client doesn't know it, that would be a complete surprise to you?" Watch the reaction, not just the words.

2. "Is there anything you already know is broken that you were hoping I'd find on my own, so you didn't have to be the one to say it first?"
   *Probe:* Give them room — if they deflect, come back to it later in the conversation rather than pushing immediately: "I'll come back to that one."

3. "Of what I'm about to tell you, what needs to be said explicitly in a document or a meeting, and what would you rather I just start fixing quietly?"
   *Probe:* If they say "just fix everything quietly," push back once: "Some of this — a Red account the client doesn't know about, a key-person risk on your biggest client — I don't think I can sit on. Where's the line for you?"

4. "If I name a political risk — a person, an incentive, an informal escalation path — how do you want that handled? Directly with them, or through you first?"
   *Probe:* If "just tell me and I'll deal with it," clarify explicitly: "So I shouldn't raise it directly with [person] myself?" Get this boundary unambiguous before you leave the room, not after you've already acted on an assumption.

5. "Now that I've told you what I found — does any of it change your view of who on the leadership team you trust most on delivery matters?"
   *Probe:* If they deflect to "I trust everyone," don't let the question disappear — it may matter more in month three than it does today; note it and return to it later.

6. "What's the one thing from this diagnostic you'd be most uncomfortable seeing in a written report, even an internal one?"
   *Probe:* This surfaces what they consider genuinely sensitive versus merely embarrassing. If they name something you were planning to write up plainly, that's a negotiation to have now, not after you've sent it.

7. "Given what I've found, is the timeline or scope you originally gave me still realistic, or does this change what you want from me in the next quarter?"
   *Probe:* If "no change, keep going as planned," and what you found includes a structural gap (no renewal mechanism, no succession plan, a Delivery Director already over-loaded past ~10 Delivery Owners), name the tension plainly: "I want to flag that fixing that specific thing is multi-quarter, not something I can land inside the original plan without slowing something else down."

8. "Is there anything I've told you today that you don't believe, or think I've got wrong?"
   *Probe:* This is the most important question in the whole set, and the one people skip. If they wave it off ("no, sounds right"), press once: "Even the account I flagged as effectively Red — you're not more optimistic about that one than I am?" You want disagreement surfaced now, in private, not three weeks later in a meeting where you look like you got it wrong.

## Using these scripts responsibly

Scripts 1-5 rely on people being candid about problems they would not otherwise raise with a new leader in the room. That candour only holds if it's protected:

- Do not attribute a specific quote, complaint, or admission from a frontline, Delivery Owner, or Resource Manager conversation back to the individual in any written output — including your own notes if they might later be shared, your week 4 synthesis, or the leadership conversation in Script 6. Where a pattern needs to be reported, describe the pattern (as with client identity and commercially sensitive terms in [knowledge-management.md](knowledge-management.md)), not the source.
- Use variances and gaps you uncover to calibrate your own understanding and future process, not to attribute blame to the person who disclosed them — the same principle Stage 4 applies to timesheet variance review in [stage-4-delivery-to-finance-transition.md](stage-4-delivery-to-finance-transition.md).
- Script 6 is the one place attribution is expected and appropriate — you are calibrating with a named sponsor, on the record, by design. Do not let disclosures from Scripts 1-5 leak into that conversation in a form that identifies who said what.
- If a conversation surfaces something you are obliged to act on immediately (a live client-facing risk, a safety or conduct issue), say so to the person in the room before you do it. Confidentiality protects candour; it is not a mechanism for withholding something that needs to be raised regardless of source.
